# Tag, You're It — full-game roster catalogue

> **DRAFT — for Avery's keep / cut / swap reaction.** This is raw research material, not game copy: habitat, depth, diet, size, IUCN status, distinctive facts, and migration notes for ~45 candidate species. The six current roster sharks (nurse, thresher, whale, goblin, tiger, sand tiger) are excluded — they're done. Avery does the field-note writing and voice; this is the factual bedrock underneath.
>
> Every dossier was researched against current sources (Oct 2026). Where sources conflict or data is thin, it's flagged in **Flags** rather than smoothed over. IUCN statuses reflect the most recent assessments found — many secondary sources online still show older ones.

**Count:** 45 species across 4 ecologies. **Cut for lack of data:** none — all 45 held up.

## Pelagic hunters

### Great white shark (*Carcharodon carcharias*)
**Slot:** The apex shark — famous from *Jaws*, countershaded torpedo body, conical snout, serrated triangular teeth; the world's largest predatory fish and the flagship predator every catalogue needs
- **Habitat:** Coastal and offshore waters of all major oceans where water is 12–24°C; greatest concentrations off California, northeastern USA, South Africa, Japan, Oceania, Chile, and the Mediterranean
- **Depth:** Surface to 1,200 m; migratory legs include dives to ~900 m
- **Diet / bait:** Adults take seals, sea lions, dolphins, whale carcasses; juveniles eat fish and rays / Bait: large oily fish (tuna), marine mammal scent, whale blubber
- **Size:** Up to ~6 m (20 ft); most males 3.4–4 m, females 4.6–4.9 m average
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (assessed 2018)
- **Facts:** Keeps its swimming muscles, eyes and brain warmer than the surrounding water (regional endothermy) / Burst speeds of ~56 km/h and can breach clear of the surface when striking / Skin holds melanocytes that may let it lighten or darken its countershading for ambush camouflage
- **Migration:** California sharks leave the coast each winter for the "White Shark Café," a mid-Pacific zone between Baja California and Hawaii, spending 100+ days there (reason still unknown — feeding or mating) before returning; one tagged shark swam South Africa to northwestern Australia and back, a 20,000 km round trip in under nine months
- **Flags:** Depth max sometimes cited as ~1,875 m in one paper, but 1,200 m is the widely repeated figure — used the latter

### Shortfin mako (*Isurus oxyrinchus*)
**Slot:** The fastest shark on Earth (70 km/h) — a torpedo with long, visibly protruding teeth, famous for leaping 20 feet out of the water while hunting tuna and swordfish
- **Habitat:** Temperate and tropical seas worldwide, coastal and oceanic, between ~50°N and 50°S; highly migratory
- **Depth:** Surface to 888 m in most reports (occasional records near ~990–1,480 m in tracking studies); usually above 150 m
- **Diet / bait:** Bony fish (tuna, swordfish, mackerel, bluefish), squid, sometimes smaller sharks and marine mammals / Bait: fast oily fish (mackerel, bonito, tuna chunks)
- **Size:** About 4 m typical; verified to 4.45 m, up to ~555 kg
- **IUCN:** Endangered (assessed 2018)
- **Facts:** Name "mako" comes from the Māori word for shark / Hunts by lunging up from below, tearing off prey's fins and tails first so it can't escape / Keeps body temperature 6–8°C above ambient water, letting it acclimate to many environments
- **Migration:** Extreme wanderer: conventional tags show trans-Atlantic movements and recaptures up to 4,542 km from tagging; satellite-tracked individuals in the Southern Hemisphere logged 8,776–24,213 km cumulative (mean ~40 km/day); one named individual, "Jiffy Lube 2," looped the Atlantic twice (11,000 miles in a year, Nova Scotia to the Bahamas). Shows fidelity to certain continental-shelf areas for months at a time between ocean transits
- **Flags:** Sources conflict on IUCN pre-2019 (older pages say Vulnerable); current global status is Endangered — used that. Depth: typical max cited 888 m; a 2025 Taiwan study recorded 993.5 m

### Blue shark (*Prionace glauca*)
**Slot:** The beautiful indigo wanderer — slender, long-snouted, unmistakable deep-blue back and white belly; probably the most wide-ranging of all sharks
- **Habitat:** Temperate and tropical oceans worldwide, preferring cooler water; pelagic
- **Depth:** Surface to ~400 m, though most commonly in the upper 150 m; some records deeper
- **Diet / bait:** Small schooling fish (herring, hake, cod, mackerel) and squid; also smaller sharks, seabirds, whale carcasses / Bait: squid, oily baitfish (mackerel)
- **Size:** Typically 2–3 m; females larger, up to 3.8 m and 391 kg
- **IUCN:** Near Threatened
- **Facts:** Females bear litters of 25–135 pups, among the largest litters of any shark / Female skin is much thicker than the male's — courtship involves biting, and females can store sperm until conditions suit / One shark tagged in Ireland was recaptured 274 days later off West Africa, an estimated 6,840 km cruise
- **Migration:** Among the longest migrants of any shark: New England to South America runs are classic; tagged Irish sharks have turned up off West Africa and one was recaptured 17 years after tagging. Movements are strongly seasonal with water temperature
- **Flags:** A 2023 study proposed reclassifying it into genus *Carcharhinus* — taxonomic flux, kept the familiar name. Max size 6.1 m claims exist but were never scientifically documented

### Oceanic whitetip shark (*Carcharhinus longimanus*)
**Slot:** The open-ocean sentinel — unmistakable long, rounded, white-blotched pectoral and dorsal fins; slow, curious, bold patrols of blue-water emptiness
- **Habitat:** Circumtropical, pelagic far offshore in waters 18–28°C; occasionally near oceanic islands
- **Depth:** Surface to ~1,082 m (most sources quote 0–230 m, but recent telemetry shows dives past 1,000 m into 8°C water)
- **Diet / bait:** Pelagic bony fishes (tunas, barracuda, swordfish, oarfish), squid, marine mammals, seabirds, carrion / Bait: tuna chunks, oily fish, anything carrion-like
- **Size:** Usually ~2.7 m; up to 3.5–3.9 m, ~170 kg
- **IUCN:** Critically Endangered (uplisted from Vulnerable/Endangered in the 2019 global assessment; declines of 80–98% across much of its range)
- **Facts:** Once thought the most abundant large pelagic shark on Earth, then collapsed from finning and longline bycatch / Its fins are among the fourteen species most common in Hong Kong fin markets / Frequently swims with pilot fish (*Naucrates ductor*) alongside
- **Migration:** Philopatric migrations in the northern Atlantic between shallow reef habitats and oceanic waters; site fidelity documented off northeast Brazil; generally highly migratory but movement data are still thin for much of its range
- **Flags:** Older sources (pre-2019) still say "Vulnerable" — current IUCN status is Critically Endangered, confirmed across multiple recent sources

### Silky shark (*Carcharhinus falciformis*)
**Slot:** Named for its unusually smooth, silk-like skin — slender and quick, the shark that shadows tuna schools and gathers around fish aggregating devices (FADs)
- **Habitat:** Circumtropical, open ocean and near the edges of continental/insular shelves; prefers water ~23–24°C
- **Depth:** Surface to 500 m; most consistently near the shelf edge in the upper 50 m
- **Diet / bait:** Pelagic bony fishes (tuna, albacore, mullet, sea catfish), squid, crabs / Bait: tuna, oily schooling fish, squid
- **Size:** Up to 3.3–3.5 m
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (global; Near Threatened in some regional assessments)
- **Facts:** Second-most harvested shark species globally; its fins are ~3.5% of the Hong Kong market / Accounts for over 90% of shark bycatch in tropical tuna purse-seine fisheries around FADs / Juvenile silkies have been satellite-tracked traveling thousands of km, routinely leaving even a 133,000 km² marine reserve (Galápagos) — less than half their time inside it
- **Migration:** Highly migratory with thousands-of-kilometres tracks; genetic studies suggest female site fidelity to pupping areas even as individuals range widely
- **Flags:** Sources conflict on IUCN: older says Near Threatened, most current (Rigby et al. 2021 assessment, CMS) say Vulnerable — used Vulnerable with regional nuance noted

### Porbeagle (*Lamna nasus*)
**Slot:** The cold-water athlete — a mini-great-white of chilly northern seas; the porbeagle's white trailing edge on the dorsal fin is its ID badge
- **Habitat:** Cold and temperate waters of the North Atlantic, Mediterranean, and the southern hemisphere (south Australia, South Africa, southern South America), 30–60°S
- **Depth:** Surface to 700+ m (715 m in ID guides)
- **Diet / bait:** Small pelagic bony fishes (herring, mackerel, cod, haddock, lancetfish), squid; juveniles eat more squid and soft-bodied prey / Bait: oily schooling fish (mackerel, herring), squid
- **Size:** Usually ~2 m; maximum ~3–3.7 m
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (global; Critically Endangered in the Northeast Atlantic, Endangered in the Northwest Atlantic — strong regional split)
- **Facts:** A tagged juvenile swam over 2,400 km in 122 days (Ireland to Morocco) — long migrations start young / Females migrate thousands of miles to mating grounds off Maine/Newfoundland in early autumn / Was one of Europe's most commercially valuable sharks in the early 1900s, and some North Atlantic populations fell to ~10% of original numbers
- **Migration:** Strong seasonal return migrations: NE Atlantic porbeagles tagged at the Bay of Biscay traveled up to 2,000 km (to the Arctic Circle, Madeira, mid-Atlantic Ridge) and returned to the tagging point the following spring — 5,000–13,000 km annual loops with clear site fidelity; up to 100 km/day transit bursts
- **Flags:** Regional statuses differ sharply (Vulnerable global vs. Critically Endangered NE Atlantic) — the split is real and worth showing in game

### Salmon shark (*Lamna ditropis*)
**Slot:** The Pacific's porbeagle cousin, named for what it eats — a heavy-bodied, warm-blooded salmon hunter of the North Pacific, shy on scuba but bolder near trawled bait
- **Habitat:** North Pacific Ocean only: Bering Sea to Baja California in the east, Japan to the Sea of Okhotsk in the west; cool coastal and oceanic waters 2–24°C
- **Depth:** Surface to ~1,864 m (deepest report); typically epipelagic, most records above ~225 m
- **Diet / bait:** Pacific salmon above all, plus herring, sardines, walleye pollock, squid, sablefish, occasionally seals, sea otters, birds / Bait: salmon, herring, squid
- **Size:** Up to ~3.05 m
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** One 1998 study estimated salmon sharks ate 12–25% of the entire annual Prince William Sound salmon run / In summer they cruise just under the surface in Prince William Sound, dorsal fins exposed in slow circles / Sex-segregated by ocean side: males more common in the west, females in the east. Partial endotherm — maintains stomach/eye/brain temperature, hunting effectively in very cold water
- **Migration:** Seasonally migratory, following prey: summer in the north (Gulf of Alaska, Prince William Sound, Kuril Islands), winter south to Japan in the west or Oregon/California coasts in the east; some stay in the Gulf of Alaska year-round; averages ~33 km/day on migratory legs
- **Flags:** Older sources list Data Deficient (Animal Diversity Web); current IUCN assessment is Least Concern — flag the dated conflict. Depth records conflict wildly (668 m vs 1,864 m); gave both with typical range

### Copper shark / bronze whaler (*Carcharhinus brachyurus*)
**Slot:** The sardine-run star — a big bronze requiem shark that hunts schooling fish in coordinated groups off South Africa, center-stage in BBC *Blue Planet* baitball footage
- **Habitat:** Warm-temperate coastal waters: southwestern and northeastern Atlantic, southern Africa, northwestern and eastern Pacific, Australia, New Zealand; brackish rivers and estuaries to offshore waters
- **Depth:** Surface to ~100 m (IUCN assessment cites to 145 m); recorded max ~100 m
- **Diet / bait:** Schooling pelagic fish — sardines, anchovies, pilchards, mackerel, mullet — plus squid, smaller sharks, rays / Bait: sardines, pilchards, schooling baitfish
- **Size:** Up to 3.3 m
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (2020 assessment, Vulnerable A2bd)
- **Facts:** The dominant large predator at South Africa's annual Sardine Run, driving baitballs upward while dolphins and gannets attack from above / Females don't mature until ~20 years and pup only every 2–3 years — among the slowest reproduction of any coastal carcharhinid / Males and females live apart for most of the year
- **Migration:** Seasonal coastwise migrations: South African tagging shows movements over 1,000 km along the South African and Mozambique coasts in a single migration cycle; females make seasonal movements between coastal nursery bays and offshore waters
- **Flags:** "Bronze whaler" vs "copper shark" both in wide use — same species, no conflict. One low-quality source called it "endangered" (generic), but IUCN 2020 says Vulnerable — used that

### Great hammerhead (*Sphyrna mokarran*)
**Slot:** The biggest hammerhead — its wide T-shaped head carries only slight indentations, plus a towering sickle-shaped first dorsal fin; the ray-hunter
- **Habitat:** Circumglobal tropical and warm-temperate seas, coastal-pelagic and semi-oceanic: continental shelves, island terraces, coral atoll passes, close inshore to well offshore
- **Depth:** Near-surface to 80+ m; tagged sharks spent 75% of records above 30 m
- **Diet / bait:** Rays and skates above all (stingrays pinned with the head, then flipped), plus other sharks, bony fish, cephalopods / Bait: ray, oily fish chunks
- **Size:** Up to 5.5–6.1 m; mature adults typically ~4–4.5 m
- **IUCN:** Critically Endangered (global; assessed 2019)
- **Facts:** Uses the hammer's underside sensors to find stingrays buried in sand, pins them to the seafloor, then works them into its mouth — stingray spines are routinely found embedded in hammerheads / Its fins are among the most valued in the shark fin trade / Particularly fragile when caught — may not survive even if released
- **Migration:** Nomadic and highly migratory: confirmed long-distance moves (e.g. Bahamas to South Carolina in pregnant females, parturition off the US East Coast); strong site fidelity patterns are regional and still being worked out; shows a diel depth cycle, deeper by day
- **Flags:** One scuba-guide source says "Endangered" (outdated); current global IUCN assessment is Critically Endangered — used that

### Scalloped hammerhead (*Sphyrna lewini*)
**Slot:** The schooling hammerhead — the "scalloped" front edge of its hammer distinguishes it, and it's the species that forms vast mysterious schools at Galápagos, Cocos, and Malpelo
- **Habitat:** Warm-temperate and tropical coastal and offshore waters worldwide (46°N–36°S); inshore by day, offshore at night; young in coastal nurseries
- **Depth:** Surface to 500 m; most commonly 25–100 m
- **Diet / bait:** Schooling bony fish (sardines, herring, mackerel, barracuda, conger eels), stingrays, squid, octopus, crustaceans, smaller sharks / Bait: sardines, squid, oily fish
- **Size:** Up to 3.7–4.3 m; females larger than males
- **IUCN:** Critically Endangered (global; assessed 2019)
- **Facts:** The hammer's wide-spread electroreceptors (ampullae of Lorenzini) let it detect stingrays buried under sediment — ray spines are often found embedded in its mouth / Pups are born at just 30–55 cm in shallow coastal nurseries and face heavy predation / Young gather in huge schools of up to hundreds; why remains a genuine mystery
- **Migration:** Migratory with pronounced nursery-to-offshore life cycle: pupped in coastal lagoons, adults move offshore; large schooling migrations are famous at the Galápagos Marine Reserve, where nursery sites were only confirmed in 2017
- **Flags:** Older university fact sheets still say Near Threatened; current IUCN is Critically Endangered — used the current status

### Smooth hammerhead (*Sphyrna zygaena*)
**Slot:** The cool-water hammerhead — second-largest of the family, with a smooth, un-notched front edge; the most oceanic and temperature-tolerant hammerhead
- **Habitat:** Worldwide tropical and temperate waters; prefers cooler water than its cousins; coastal shelves, inshore bays (nurseries) to well offshore
- **Depth:** Surface to at least 200 m; prefers 0–20 m in warm areas, deeper in the tropics
- **Diet / bait:** Bony fishes, small sharks, skates, stingrays, cephalopods / Bait: oily fish, squid, ray
- **Size:** Up to 4–5 m; usually 2.5–3 m
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable
- **Facts:** Records the longest hammerhead migration ever documented: over 6,600 km across hemispheres (Santos & Coelho 2018) / It eats other sharks — including smaller hammerhead species / Juveniles sometimes travel in huge migrating schools in the eastern Pacific
- **Migration:** Strongly migratory across international borders: north-south seasonal movements (poleward in summer following cool water masses), inshore-offshore legs; schools of hundreds to thousands reported in summer
- **Flags:** Sources agree on Vulnerable; depth records vary (55 m to 200 m) depending on source vintage — gave the wider verified range

### Bonnethead (*Sphyrna tiburo*)
**Slot:** The shovel-headed omnivore — the smallest hammerhead, with a smooth spade-like head; the only shark confirmed to digest seagrass, and the only hammerhead with sex-dimorphic head shape
- **Habitat:** Continental and insular shelves of the Americas: western Atlantic (North Carolina to southern Brazil, Gulf of Mexico, Caribbean) and eastern Pacific (southern California to Ecuador); estuaries, shallow bays, seagrass flats, coral reefs
- **Depth:** Shore to 90 m; usually 10–25 m
- **Diet / bait:** Chiefly crustaceans — especially blue crabs — plus molluscs, small fish, and deliberately eaten seagrass (digested at ~50% efficiency; functionally omnivorous) / Bait: crabs, shrimp, small shellfish
- **Size:** Typically 80–90 cm; max ~150 cm
- **IUCN:** Endangered (assessed 2020)
- **Facts:** Has the shortest gestation of any shark — just 4–5 months / A female at Omaha's Henry Doorly Zoo reproduced by parthenogenesis (virgin birth), with DNA confirming an exact maternal match / No bite on a human is recorded in the Global Shark Attack File — harmless
- **Migration:** Seasonal latitudinal movements following water temperature: equatorward in winter, back to higher latitudes in summer, often in small schools; shows considerable sexual segregation
- **Flags:** Older papers cite a "Least Concern" IUCN listing; the 2020 global reassessment is Endangered (driven by Pacific/Caribbean disappearances, e.g. absent from Mexico since the 1980s, last Gulf of California record decades old) — used Endangered with the dated conflict flagged

## Reef & coastal

### Galápagos shark (*Carcharhinus galapagensis*)
**Slot:** The island shark — bold, stocky reef giant with a tall rounded dorsal fin, almost always found in groups around remote oceanic islands, and easily confused with the grey reef and dusky sharks.
- **Habitat:** Circumglobal tropical waters, almost exclusively around oceanic islands and seamounts (Socorro, Clipperton, Cocos, Malpelo, Galápagos, Hawaiian chain, Madagascar, West Africa); absent from the northern Indian Ocean; rarely inshore.
- **Depth:** Surface to ~285 m; juveniles stay in shallow water to avoid adult predation.
- **Diet / bait:** Bottom-dwelling bony fishes and cephalopods; large individuals eat other sharks, marine iguanas, sea lions — bait: reef fish or squid chunks.
- **Size:** Typically 3.0 m; max ~3.7 m.
- **IUCN:** Least Concern.
- **Facts:** Identifying mark is the tall first dorsal fin with a rounded tip originating over the rear of the pectoral fins / Juveniles hide in shallow water because the adults will eat sharks too — nursery waters are real refuges / Despite being an island specialist, it is the most abundant shark at many remote archipelagos.
- **Migration:** Aggregates around food-rich islands and seamounts; inter-island movement appears limited but is thinly documented.
- **Flags:** IUCN conflict — older sources list Near Threatened; the 2019 assessment (current per Wikipedia/sharksandrays) downgraded to Least Concern. Island-to-island movement data is thin.

### Bull shark (*Carcharhinus leucas*)
**Slot:** The river shark — the only big requiem shark that casually swims hundreds of kilometres up rivers, giving the player a freshwater/estuary expedition option no other species offers.
- **Habitat:** Tropical and subtropical coastal waters worldwide plus rivers and estuaries — documented in the Mississippi, Amazon, Gambia, Ganges, Tigris, Zambezi, San Juan/Lake Nicaragua, and Swan River systems.
- **Depth:** Mostly ~30 m; recorded to 164 m in marine waters; juveniles in rivers and estuaries.
- **Diet / bait:** Generalist — fish, rays, small sharks, crustaceans, turtles, birds, mammals; bait: mullet, bonito, or stingray.
- **Size:** Typically 2.2–3.5 m; max ~4 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** Euryhaline — it recycles its own urea and regulates blood salt to thrive in both salt and fresh water / One bull shark was documented ~1,100 km up the Mississippi River, near Alton, Illinois / Short blunt snout and a stocky "bull" build; young sharks have black-tipped fins that fade with age.
- **Migration:** Long coastal movements; juveniles spend years in estuary and freshwater nurseries before moving offshore; tracked individuals shift between salt and fresh water seasonally.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the 2021 reassessment (Rigby et al.) upgraded to Vulnerable. Max size varies by source (3.4–4 m).

### Lemon shark (*Negaprion brevirostris*)
**Slot:** The yellow nursery shark — unmistakable by its two nearly equal-sized dorsal fins and lemon-yellow tint, and the species with the most famous natal philopatry in shark science (decades of Bimini tagging).
- **Habitat:** Western Atlantic (New Jersey to southern Brazil, Caribbean, Gulf of Mexico), eastern Atlantic (Mauritania to Angola), eastern Pacific (Baja California to Ecuador); shallow continental shelves, mangroves, bays.
- **Depth:** Surface to 92 m; juveniles in very shallow mangrove lagoons.
- **Diet / bait:** Mostly bony fishes and crustaceans; night feeder using electroreception — bait: sardines or mullet, best at night.
- **Size:** Typically 2.4–3.1 m; max ~3.4 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** Both dorsal fins are nearly the same size — the quickest ID feature in the catalogue / Females return to the exact nursery where they were born to give birth, and mate with multiple males (polyandry) / Lifespan is 25–30+ years; one lived to 40 in captivity.
- **Migration:** Extreme natal philopatry — decades of Bimini research show females return to their own birthplace to give birth; small home ranges otherwise; adults migrate between hunting and breeding grounds.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the 2021 reassessment upgraded to Vulnerable, and it is CITES Appendix II.

### Blacktip reef shark (*Carcharhinus melanopterus*)
**Slot:** The knee-deep shark — the stereotypical "shark fin cutting the surface" image; patrols reef flats so shallow its dorsal fin breaks water, with crisp black tips on every fin.
- **Habitat:** Tropical/subtropical Indo-Pacific — Red Sea and East Africa to southern Japan, northern Australia, French Polynesia; also in the Mediterranean via the Suez Canal.
- **Depth:** Very shallow, often <20 m; to ~75 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Small reef fishes, crustaceans, cephalopods — bait: reef fish or shrimp.
- **Size:** Usually ~1.6 m; max ~2 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** Timid and skittish — frequently frightened away by swimmers, but excited by bait and known to leap out of the water when feeding / Moorea photo-ID work found stable, non-random social associations — the same individuals repeatedly choose to hang out together / Breeds in captivity and is one of the most common aquarium sharks, which shaped its reputation.
- **Migration:** Extremely small home ranges with strong site fidelity lasting years; one of the least mobile sharks in the catalogue.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the current assessment is Vulnerable and declining. Size reports vary (1.6–2 m).

### Whitetip reef shark (*Triaenodon obesus*)
**Slot:** The cave-napping shark — the only requiem shark that can pump water over its gills while lying still, so the player finds it piled in heaps in reef caves by day and hunting crevices by night.
- **Habitat:** Indo-Pacific coral reefs, from South Africa to Central America (Panama, Costa Rica, Cocos, Galápagos); caves, ledges, and crevices.
- **Depth:** Typically 8–40 m; one exceptional record at 330 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Reef fishes (eels, snappers, parrotfish), octopus, spiny lobster, crabs — hunts by wriggling into crevices; bait: octopus or reef-fish scraps.
- **Size:** Usually under 1.6 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** The only living species in its genus (Triaenodon) — a true evolutionary singleton / Can survive six weeks without food, and hunts in loose night groups that are not feeding frenzies — each shark hunts for itself / Off Hawaii they have been seen following Hawaiian monk seals to steal catches.
- **Migration:** Strong site fidelity — individuals stay in one reef area for months or years, repeatedly returning to the same daytime shelter.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the current assessment is Vulnerable. The 330 m record is an outlier; treat the 8–40 m band as typical.

### Grey reef shark (*Carcharhinus amblyrhynchos*)
**Slot:** The one that warns you — famous for a formal "threat display" (arched back, dropped pectorals, figure-of-eight swimming) that signals exactly how close you can get before it bites.
- **Habitat:** Tropical Indo-Pacific coral reefs, atoll passes, and lagoons — Red Sea and East Africa to Taiwan, Hawaii, Easter Island, plus Galápagos and Cocos.
- **Depth:** Surface to ~280 m (sources report extreme records to 1,000 m); typically near the reef wall.
- **Diet / bait:** Bony reef fish, squid, octopus, crabs, lobsters, shrimp — bait: mackerel or squid.
- **Size:** Usually under 1.9 m; max ~2.5 m.
- **IUCN:** Endangered.
- **Facts:** Its threat display is a fixed, readable warning — almost every diver bite has followed an ignored display, like a rattlesnake's rattle / Identified by a broad black band along the trailing edge of the tail fin / Grey reef sharks perform a daily tidal commute, moving inshore as the tide ebbs and retreating deeper as it rises.
- **Migration:** High site fidelity to reef stretches, but recent tracking shows trips over 100 km across open ocean between reefs — more mobile than once thought.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the 2020 assessment upgraded to Endangered. Depth ranges conflict widely between sources (280 m vs 1,000 m).

### Caribbean reef shark (*Carcharhinus perezi*)
**Slot:** The Bahamas dive shark — the Caribbean's most common reef shark, the star of shark-feeding dives, and the species whose tracking data proved marine protected areas work.
- **Habitat:** Tropical western Atlantic — Florida to Brazil, Bermuda, northern Gulf of Mexico, and throughout the Caribbean Sea; reef drop-offs.
- **Depth:** Usually under 30 m; recorded dives to 378 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Reef fishes (including bigeyes) and sometimes other elasmobranchs — bait: bony reef fish or fish chum.
- **Size:** Typically 2–2.5 m; max ~2.95 m.
- **IUCN:** Endangered.
- **Facts:** Known in Spanish as "cabeza dura" — thick skull / One of the most studied sharks in the world, and key to evaluating whether marine sanctuaries actually protect sharks / Shows tonic immobility — gentle sensory manipulation can put it into a relaxed, immobile trance.
- **Migration:** Strong site fidelity to reef areas with limited large-scale migration — which is exactly why marine reserves protect them well.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; the 2021 assessment (Carlson et al.) upgraded to Endangered. Note the common misspelling "C. perezii" in some sources.

### Sandbar shark (*Carcharhinus plumbeus*)
**Slot:** The high-fin shark — identified by its unusually tall, triangular first dorsal fin, a rounded snout, and large pectoral fins; the sandy-bay specialist that avoids surf zones and coral reefs.
- **Habitat:** Circumglobal in tropical and temperate waters — western Atlantic (Rhode Island to Venezuela, Brazil to Uruguay), eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean, western Indian Ocean, Southeast Asia, Australia, Hawaii, Galápagos; nearly absent from the eastern Pacific.
- **Depth:** Shoreline to 280 m; adults most common at 20–50 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Small bony fishes (menhaden, kingfish, mackerel, snappers), small sharks and rays, molluscs, crustaceans; juveniles eat crabs and mantis shrimp — bait: menhaden, mackerel, or squid.
- **Size:** Typically 1.8–2.4 m; max ~3 m.
- **IUCN:** Endangered.
- **Facts:** Females grow thicker skin to protect themselves during mating / Age-segregated — nurseries of young sharks use sheltered bays, away from the adults / Despite being a large shark it is considered not particularly dangerous because of its strong preference for fish and invertebrate prey.
- **Migration:** Some populations make long seasonal migrations tied to water temperature and reproduction; nurseries are geographically separate from adult grounds.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Vulnerable; the 2021 assessment upgraded to Endangered. Maximum size varies by source (240–300 cm).

### Dusky shark (*Carcharhinus obscurus*)
**Slot:** The slowest-lived shark in the game — females mature at ~20, gestate for nearly two years, and breed only every third year, making it the catalogue's best story about why sharks are vulnerable to fishing.
- **Habitat:** Tropical and temperate continental coastlines worldwide (western Atlantic, western Indian Ocean, eastern Pacific); surf zone to outer continental shelf; avoids estuaries.
- **Depth:** Surface to 400 m; most tracking time at 10–80 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Sardines, eels, tunas, skates, squid, smaller sharks, whale carcasses, garbage — bait: sardine, squid, or mackerel.
- **Size:** To ~4 m (13 ft).
- **IUCN:** Endangered.
- **Facts:** 22–24 month gestation — matched among vertebrates only by elephants and a few deep-sea sharks — followed by a full resting year, so one litter every three years / Young dusky sharks form large feeding schools, and one group of 10–20 was observed attacking a humpback whale calf off South Africa / Adults are among the largest of their genus, yet have no single field mark — identification is by proportion, including a skin ridge between the dorsal fins.
- **Migration:** Strongly migratory — recorded movements up to 3,800 km, with seasonal poleward shifts; but females show natal site fidelity, so local populations stay genetically distinct.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Vulnerable; current assessment is Endangered. Reported weight varies wildly by source (350–400 lb vs up to 350 kg); use length only.

### Silvertip shark (*Carcharhinus albimarginatus*)
**Slot:** The heavyweight bully — a bulkier, white-edged cousin of the grey reef shark that dominates equally sized sharks at food, with battle-scarred adults whose fin notches identify individuals without tags.
- **Habitat:** Tropical Indo-Pacific offshore islands, coral reefs, seamounts, and banks — fragmented; notably absent from the Red Sea and Hawaii.
- **Depth:** Surface to 800 m; patrols the water column off steep walls rather than sitting on the reef.
- **Diet / bait:** Midwater and bottom bony fishes, eagle rays, smaller sharks, cephalopods — bait: mackerel, tuna, or squid.
- **Size:** To 3 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** Where it meets Galápagos or blacktip sharks, it displaces them at food; among themselves, rank is settled by ramming and biting — which is why big individuals are heavily scarred / Often follows boats, approaches divers closely, and is extremely bold in baited situations / Suspected nurseries exist at remote offshore islands like Socorro, where juveniles visit cleaning stations.
- **Migration:** Island-associated with limited dispersal between sites; young stay in shallow inshore water while adults range more widely.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened (Shark Research Institute lists Not Evaluated); current IUCN assessment is Vulnerable. Some sources report local populations already extirpated.

### Spinner shark (*Carcharhinus brevipinna*)
**Slot:** The acrobat — corkscrews vertically through bait schools and exits the water spinning, up to three full rotations in the air, in a hunting leap that is pure attack momentum, not play.
- **Habitat:** Tropical and warm-temperate coastal and offshore waters worldwide except the eastern Pacific; open coastal water and current lines rather than the reef proper.
- **Depth:** Surface to 100 m; usually under 30 m.
- **Diet / bait:** Schooling forage fish (sardines, herring), small tunas, sea catfish, stingrays, squid — bait: live sardines or herring.
- **Size:** Average ~2 m; max ~3 m.
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable.
- **Facts:** The spinning leap is the exit of an attack run that starts ~20 m down — it needs dense schooling prey to work, which is why this is an open-water animal / Easily confused with the blacktip shark, but the first dorsal fin sits further back and adults have a black tip on the anal fin (blacktips don't) / Swift, gregarious, and can get belligerent around speared fish.
- **Migration:** Migratory — schooling behavior and seasonal movements documented in the Gulf of Mexico; litters of up to 20 pups are born every other year in shallow coastal nurseries.
- **Flags:** Older sources list Near Threatened; current IUCN assessment is Vulnerable.

### Epaulette shark (*Hemiscyllium ocellatum*)
**Slot:** The walking shark — a metre-long tide-pool shark that "walks" between rock pools on paddle-like fins and survives total oxygen loss for two hours; the only oviparous shark in the catalogue.
- **Habitat:** Shallow tropical reefs and tidal pools of northern Australia and New Guinea (Great Barrier Reef focus).
- **Depth:** Intertidal zone to ~40 m; often found in 15 cm of water.
- **Diet / bait:** Worms, crabs, shrimp, and small fishes — bait: worms or crabs.
- **Size:** Usually under 1 m; max 107 cm.
- **IUCN:** Least Concern.
- **Facts:** Survives complete anoxia for two hours by flooding the brain with blood and shutting down non-essential neural functions — a superpower now studied for stroke treatment / Named for the large white-ringed black spot behind each pectoral fin, like military epaulettes — eyespots that may deflect predator attacks / Forages at low tide when the reef top is exposed and larger predatory sharks have retreated to deeper water.
- **Migration:** Essentially sedentary — tiny home ranges, walks between tide pools; no real migration.
- **Flags:** The Papua New Guinea subpopulation is assessed Near Threatened due to dynamite fishing and pollution; a recent taxonomic split may restrict H. ocellatum to the Great Barrier Reef with the PNG form described separately, so range statements are uncertain.

## Benthic oddballs & filter feeders

### Spotted wobbegong (*Orectolobus maculatus*)
**Slot:** A living carpet — flattened ambush predator with O-shaped spots, fleshy beard-like lobes around the mouth, and a bite that holds on (divers call it the "settle down, you'll regret stepping on it" shark). Endemic to Australia.
- **Habitat:** Coastal Australia — east coast from Gladstone (Queensland) southward, most of the south coast, west coast north to Western Australia; absent from Tasmania. Rocky/coral reefs, sandy bottoms, seagrass beds, estuaries, tide pools.
- **Depth:** Intertidal to 218 m
- **Diet / bait:** Nocturnal ambush predator; bony fishes, smaller sharks and rays, crabs, lobsters, octopuses. Suction-feeds — lunges and inhales prey whole. Bait: fish chunks or squid presented near the bottom at night.
- **Size:** Typically 150–180 cm; reported up to ~3.2 m
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** "Wobbegong" likely derives from an Australian Aboriginal word meaning "shaggy beard" / Can crawl out of the water on its pectoral fins to drag itself between tide pools / Prey have been seen nibbling its sensory barbels moments before it inhales them
- **Migration:** Not a long-distance migrant; telemetry shows strong seasonal site fidelity — individuals returned to the same coral reef five years running
- **Flags:** Max-size figures conflict: Australian field sources give 150–180 cm, while some cite ~320 cm; the larger records likely reflect past confusion with *Orectolobus halei* (Gulf/banded wobbegong), split as a separate species in 2008. Japanese/South China Sea records are considered misidentifications.

### Zebra shark (*Stegostoma tigrinum*)
**Slot:** The shape-shifter — pups are boldly zebra-striped, adults are leopard-spotted, and early scientists named them as two different species. Five body ridges and a tail nearly half its body length. One of the most documented cases of "virgin birth" in a vertebrate.
- **Habitat:** Tropical Indo-Pacific: Red Sea, Persian Gulf, Indian Ocean, western Pacific. Sandy flats and coral reef slopes.
- **Depth:** Intertidal to ~62 m (some sources list up to ~90 m); striped juveniles are rarely seen and may hide deeper
- **Diet / bait:** Molluscs (snails), crustaceans, small bony fishes, possibly sea snakes; wriggles its long body into tight reef crevices and sucks prey out. Bait: crab or mollusc presented at night near reef structure.
- **Size:** Typically ~2.5 m; recorded to 3.5 m
- **IUCN:** Endangered (global assessment 2023)
- **Facts:** Pup stripes are thought to mimic venomous sea snakes so predators keep their distance / Females lay dozens of large egg cases anchored to rock by adhesive fiber tufts / A female kept isolated for years switched from normal breeding to viable asexual reproduction — the first documented such switch in a shark
- **Migration:** Mostly solitary but forms large seasonal aggregations (e.g., Byron Bay, Australia); undergoes extensive annual migrations of up to 2,000 km along the Australian coast
- **Flags:** Regional status differs: assessed Least Concern in Australia, where it remains relatively abundant. A dive-industry source mistakenly called it "Vulnerable" — the current global IUCN status is Endangered. Also sold as "leopard shark" in Australia/UK — do not confuse with *Triakis semifasciata*.

### Leopard shark (*Triakis semifasciata*)
**Slot:** The California bay shark — slender, elegant, instantly recognizable by black saddle markings that look like a saddle blanket draped over its back. Forms large schools that cruise shallow mudflats with the tide.
- **Habitat:** Northeast Pacific coast: Oregon to Mazatlán, Mexico, including the Gulf of California. Shallow enclosed bays, estuaries, mudflats, sandy shores, kelp beds, rocky reefs.
- **Depth:** Intertidal to ~91 m (FishBase lists 0–156 m); most abundant in water <4 m — a genuine ankle-deep shark
- **Diet / bait:** Bottom forager: clams, crabs, shrimp, worms, small bony fishes, fish eggs (e.g., herring eggs). Mouth built for picking and crushing. Bait: clam necks, shrimp, or fish eggs in shallow bays; follow the tide onto mudflats.
- **Size:** Typically 1.2–1.5 m; max ~1.8–2.0 m (females larger than males)
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Follows the rising tide onto intertidal mudflats to feed, then retreats with it / Seven genetically distinct subpopulations have been identified along the California coast alone / Females aggregate in warm shallow water to raise their body temperature and speed digestion and growth
- **Migration:** Largely resident with low dispersal; most individuals stay in one area, though some travel up to ~140–150 km, and northern populations leave coastal bays in winter when water drops below ~10–12 °C
- **Flags:** "Leopard shark" is also used colloquially for the zebra shark (*Stegostoma tigrinum*) in Australia and Thailand — this dossier is strictly the North American *Triakis semifasciata*.

### Horn shark (*Heterodontus francisci*)
**Status: PINNED — deferred by Avery 2026-10-03 (not cut, revisit later)**
**Slot:** The bullhead — blunt pig-like snout, bony ridges over its eyes, and a sharp spine in front of each dorsal fin. Daytime sleeper wedged into crevices; nighttime crusher of hard-shelled prey with molar-like teeth.
- **Habitat:** Northeastern Pacific coast: California (as far north as San Francisco in summer) to the Gulf of California/Sea of Cortez. Rocky reefs, kelp forests, shallow bays.
- **Depth:** Intertidal to at least 152 m; adults usually on shallow rocky reefs or algal beds at 2–11 m (often <10 m). Juveniles segregate to deeper sandy flats. Winter movement to deeper water suspected.
- **Diet / bait:** Adults crush hard-shelled molluscs, echinoderms (sea urchins), and crustaceans between molar-like rear teeth; juveniles take softer prey (polychaete worms, anemones, small clams). Bait: crab or sea urchin flesh at night near rocky reef.
- **Size:** Typically ~1 m; max ~1.2 m
- **IUCN:** Data Deficient
- **Facts:** (thin documentation — only core traits confirmed) Nocturnal crusher with molar-like rear teeth; dorsal-fin spines; wedges into crevices by day
- **Migration:** Suspected winter movement to deeper water; otherwise resident on home reefs
- **Flags:** Current IUCN status is Data Deficient (older assessment, no update found); the California population is thought stable but data are thin. Maximum size: some sources give 1.0 m average, sharksandrays.com gives 1.2 m max — use ~1–1.2 m.

### Port Jackson shark (*Heterodontus portusjacksoni*)
**Slot:** The harness-wearing traveler — grey-brown with dark harness-like stripes crossing its eyes and body, pig-like blunt head with forehead ridges, spined dorsal fins. Australia's largest bullhead shark and a genuine seasonal migrant.
- **Habitat:** Coastal southern Australia (continental shelf, from Western Australia to New South Wales), plus Tasmania; occasional vagrant records in New Zealand. Rocky reefs, adjacent sand and seagrass areas.
- **Depth:** 0–275 m; usually <100 m. Daytime in caves and rocky gullies; night foraging.
- **Diet / bait:** Nocturnal; primarily benthic invertebrates — sea urchins, starfish, crabs, barnacles, polychaete worms — plus small fishes; gripped by cusped front teeth, crushed by rear molars. Bait: urchin or crab meat at night near reef.
- **Size:** To 1.65–1.7 m; commonly under 1 m
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Screws its spiral-flanged egg cases into rock crevices by holding them in its mouth and rotating its body / Migrates south in summer and returns north to breed in winter / Dorsal-fin spines are venomous — painful when handled
- **Migration:** True seasonal oceanodromous migrant: south in summer, north to breeding grounds in winter; returns annually to the same spawning sites. After breeding, males move offshore/deep first, females follow in late September–October.
- **Flags:** None significant; distribution sources are consistent (southern Australia endemic). Note dorsal spines are venomous per the Australian taxonomy source — worth a care flag in field notes.

### Common angelshark (*Squatina squatina*)
**Slot:** The flattened ghost — half shark, half ray, that vanishes into the sand and explodes upward to ambush passing fish. Once ranged from Norway to the Mediterranean; now Critically Endangered and locally extinct across much of that range.
- **Habitat:** Northeast Atlantic (Scotland to the Canary Islands, possibly down the West African coast) and Mediterranean; historically to central Norway — now regionally extinct north and east of the British Isles. Soft substrates: sand, mud, gravel; reefs, seagrass beds, lagoons, estuaries.
- **Depth:** Intertidal to at least 150 m
- **Diet / bait:** Ambush predator of demersal bony fishes — flatfish, hake, seabreams — plus skates, squid, cuttlefish, crabs. (One stomach held an entire cormorant.) Bait: whole small fish fished on/near the bottom at night.
- **Size:** To 2.4 m (females); males to ~1.8 m
- **IUCN:** Critically Endangered
- **Facts:** Buries itself by day with only eyes and dorsal fins protruding, then swims actively in the water column at night / Gestation 8–12 months for just 7–25 pups — the slow reproduction that makes it so vulnerable / Historically prized for meat, sandpaper-like skin, and liver oil; now mainly threatened as trawl/set-net bycatch
- **Migration:** Generally not a long-distance ocean traveler, but shows seasonal north–south and inshore–offshore movement; Irish-tagged individuals were recaptured in Northern Ireland, southern England, France, and Spain. Newer evidence suggests summer movement into shallow southern North Sea habitats for reproduction.
- **Flags:** Modern range is fragmented and patchy (only regular stronghold: Canary Islands); "migration" data are still described as poorly understood by researchers.

### Broadnose sevengill shark (*Notorynchus cepedianus*)
**Slot:** The seven-gilled primitive — named for seven gill slits (most sharks have five) and a single dorsal fin; an ancient lineage (Hexanchiformes) that hunts in packs and spy-hops its head out of the water.
- **Habitat:** Temperate waters worldwide: western Pacific (China, Japan, Australia, New Zealand), eastern Pacific (western North America down through Chile/Argentina), southern Atlantic (South Africa, Argentina). Continental shelves, bays, estuaries, deep bay channels.
- **Depth:** Surface to 570 m; mostly <136 m. Large adults range deeper offshore; juveniles and many adults cruise shallow bays and estuaries, sometimes in ~1 m of water.
- **Diet / bait:** Aggressive opportunistic apex predator: other sharks (including Port Jackson and leopard sharks), rays, seals, bony fish, dolphin/porpoise meat, shark egg cases, carrion. Known to hunt in packs and get aggressive around food. Bait: oily fish or marine-mammal-scented bait — though use caution given feeding aggression.
- **Size:** To ~3 m; commonly ~1.5 m; up to ~107 kg
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (2020 global assessment)
- **Facts:** Spy-hops — lifts its head clear of the water to look around / Aplacental viviparous with enormous litters of 67–104 pups / In Patagonia's Valdés Peninsula, elephant seals make up the bulk of regurgitated prey during seal abundance peaks
- **Migration:** Shows seasonal inshore–offshore movement — into estuaries (e.g., Willapa Bay, Grays Harbor, San Francisco Bay) for mating and pupping, then out to deeper water; San Diego citizen-science tracking found site-resident clusters over years
- **Flags:** Many older sources (Discover Magazine, ScubaDiving.com) still list it as Data Deficient — the current 2020 IUCN assessment is Vulnerable (suspected 30–49% global decline over three generation lengths). Use Vulnerable.

### Basking shark (*Cetorhinus maximus*)
**Slot:** The gentle giant — the world's second-largest fish (to ~11 m+), a harmless filter-feeder that swims the surface with its cavernous mouth agape, gill slits ringing its whole head, and a mottled back like barn-door planks.
- **Habitat:** Circumglobal in cold to warm-temperate seas — both North Atlantic, North Pacific, southern hemisphere (including southern Australia). Coast to the edge of the continental shelf.
- **Depth:** 0–2,000 m; at the surface feeding in spring/summer, migrating at 200–1,000 m depth, overwintering as deep as 600+ m
- **Diet / bait:** Obligate ram filter-feeder on zooplankton (copepods, krill, larvae, fish eggs) — filters ~2,000 tonnes of water per hour. No bait applies; encounters are driven by plankton blooms — "bait" in game terms should be dense zooplankton / bloom conditions.
- **Size:** Commonly 6–8 m; maximum ~10–12 m (largest reliably recorded 12.27 m, 1851)
- **IUCN:** Endangered (global; Rigby et al. 2021). Endangered in the Northeast Atlantic as well.
- **Facts:** May periodically shed and regrow its comb-like gill rakers — possibly fasting between feeding seasons / We still don't know where they mate or give birth; mating may happen far offshore in deep water / Sometimes seen in large feeding groups, and can leap clean out of the water (breaching)
- **Migration:** Highly migratory. PSAT tagging: Cape Cod to the SE coast of Brazil, over 17,000 km; some individuals showed seasonal fidelity, returning within 60 km of the tagging site a year later. Transatlantic crossings (Cape Cod toward Scotland/Ireland) also recorded.
- **Flags:** Many sources still say "Vulnerable" — that is outdated; the current global IUCN status is Endangered (2021 reassessment). Treat Vulnerable-era sources as stale.

### Megamouth shark (*Megachasma pelagios*)
**Status: KEPT — confirmed by Avery 2026-10-03 (the game's white whale)**
**Slot:** The living legend — unknown to science until a 4.5 m male tangled in Navy parachutes off Hawaii in 1976; a tadpole-shaped deep-sea filter-feeder with a vast glowing mouth, rubbery lips, and tiny hooked teeth. Only ~300 individuals ever recorded.
- **Habitat:** Cosmopolitan in tropical/temperate waters: Atlantic, Indian, and Pacific Oceans. Primarily oceanic/offshore, occasionally over continental shelves.
- **Depth:** Surface to 1,500 m; typical offshore deep water, occasionally 5–40 m over shelves
- **Diet / bait:** Filter-feeds on krill, jellyfish, shrimp/squat-lobster larvae, crab larvae — engulfs dense swarms of plankton. No bait applies; game encounters should key off deep scattering-layer conditions / plankton concentrations.
- **Size:** Typically 4–5.5 m; reported to ~7 m
- **IUCN:** Least Concern (despite rarity, assessed LC given wide range and lack of targeted fishery)
- **Facts:** (only two confirmed in this dossier — treat as thin) Discovery story (1976, Hawaii, Navy parachutes); ~300 individuals ever recorded
- **Migration:** Follows the deep scattering layer on diel vertical migration (deep by day, shallower at night)
- **Flags:** Max size is genuinely uncertain: most sources give 4–5.5 m, one MDPI review says "can reach 7 m," and a peer-reviewed paper claims "at least 820 cm TL," which looks like an outlier or error — use 4–5.5 m typical, ~7 m maximum reported. Reproductive mode is inferred (probably oophagous viviparity) but never directly observed. Diet specifics are based on a handful of stomach contents.

### Common sawshark (*Pristiophorus cirratus*)
**Slot:** The living saw — a slender shark with a long rostrum edged in 19–25 pairs of tooth-like denticles, whisker-like barbels mid-snout, and electroreceptors that detect buried prey; it rakes its saw sideways to stir up and stun dinner. (Not a sawfish — those are rays, gills underneath.)
- **Habitat:** Southern Australia (Jurien Bay, WA to Coffs Harbour, NSW), Tasmania; eastern Indian Ocean. Continental shelf and upper slope; sandy and gravelly areas offshore, occasionally bays and estuaries.
- **Depth:** 20–600 m (Fishes of Australia: 40–630 m); most commonly 37–146 m offshore; pups born shallower (~37–50 m) in warmer water
- **Diet / bait:** Primarily small crustaceans, plus squid and small fishes. Sweeps barbels over the seafloor, detects electric fields via ampullae of Lorenzini, then rakes the saw side-to-side. Bait: shrimp/crab or small squid chunks presented near the bottom.
- **Size:** Max ~1.37–1.4 m; males ~97 cm, females ~1.1 m; born 27–37 cm
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Its saw-teeth are folded flat against the snout at birth so it doesn't injure its mother / Its barbels sit slightly closer to the rostrum tip than to the mouth — the key ID mark vs. similar species / One of the fastest-maturing, most productive sharks (lives only ~15 years), which is why its population stays stable despite fishing
- **Migration:** Limited evidence of large-scale migration; some species in the family shift up/down the water column with seasonal temperature changes, but for this species, location-scale movement data are thin. Treat as mostly resident on its shelf grounds.
- **Flags:** None on core facts. Note it was formerly listed as Near Threatened; post-2000 data moved it to Least Concern, and Australian stocks are managed as Sustainable with catch quotas.

## Deep-sea & small sharks

### Greenland shark (*Somniosus microcephalus*)
**Slot:** The Methuselah of the sea — a blind, slow-motion giant that can outlive centuries.
- **Habitat:** North Atlantic and Arctic waters (Greenland, Iceland, Svalbard, northern Canada, St. Lawrence); recorded as far south as the western Caribbean
- **Depth:** Surface to ~3,000 m; shallower at high latitudes (fjords), deep offshore
- **Diet / bait:** Seals, fish (halibut, redfish, herring, eels), smaller sharks, carrion — a top Arctic predator and scavenger; bait: oily fish or marine mammal meat
- **Size:** Typically 2.4–4.3 m; can exceed 7 m, up to ~1.5 tonnes
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable
- **Facts:** World's longest-lived vertebrate — radiocarbon eye-lens dating puts lifespan at ~270 to nearly 400 years, with females not maturing until ~150 / Most individuals carry a parasitic copepod (*Ommatokoita elongata*) on their eyes and are effectively blind, hunting by smell / Its scientific name roughly means "sleepy small-head" — it cruises at a near-frozen pace
- **Migration:** Tagging shows both site fidelity and long-distance movements: recaptures up to ~1,100 km apart; satellite tags tracked ~500+ miles over a year. Inshore–offshore excursions and diel vertical migration (deeper by day, shallower at night) are documented, but seasonal patterns are still poorly understood
- **Flags:** none on core facts; max age estimate has wide error bars (±120 yrs)

### Frilled shark (*Chlamydoselachus anguineus*)
**Slot:** A living fossil — six frilled gills and an eel body, like a sea serpent from the age of dinosaurs.
- **Habitat:** Wide but patchy in the Atlantic and Pacific; outer continental shelf and upper slope, bottom-dwelling
- **Depth:** ~100–1,570 m; typically 500–1,000 m (notably shallower, 50–200 m, in Suruga Bay, Japan)
- **Diet / bait:** Mainly squid, plus bony fishes and smaller sharks; strikes by coiling and lunging like a snake — bait: squid or fish chunks
- **Size:** Up to ~2 m (female max ~196 cm)
- **IUCN:** Least Concern (global)
- **Facts:** Gestation may last up to 3.5 years — the longest of any vertebrate / Its six pairs of frilly gill slits (the first pair meeting across the throat) inspired the name and sea-serpent sightings / Its extremely flexible jaws can swallow prey half its own body size whole
- **Migration:** No evidence of long-distance migration; cold deep-water habitat likely means short bursts of activity between long periods of inactivity
- **Flags:** IUCN status conflict — assessed Near Threatened in 2016, then reclassified to Least Concern; many older sources still say Near Threatened

### Cookiecutter shark (*Isistius brasiliensis*)
**Slot:** A hand-sized glowing parasite that takes perfect circular "cookie" bites out of whales and dolphins.
- **Habitat:** Warm oceanic waters worldwide (circumtropical), especially near islands
- **Depth:** Surface to ~3,700 m; usually 200–1,000 m (mesopelagic)
- **Diet / bait:** Small deepwater fish, squid, crustaceans — and circular plugs of flesh cut from much larger animals (tuna, billfish, dolphins, seals, whales); bait: squid or fish
- **Size:** 42–56 cm (max ~50 cm)
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Its glowing green belly with a dark "collar" acts as a lure — from below it mimics the silhouette of a small prey animal / It swallows its own shed lower teeth, possibly to recycle calcium / It makes an extreme daily vertical migration: up to 3 km, rising toward the surface at dusk and sinking below 1,000 m by dawn
- **Migration:** Oceanodromous; the big pattern is diel vertical migration rather than horizontal — up to ~3,000 m of travel each day following prey toward the surface at night
- **Flags:** none on core facts

### Bluntnose sixgill shark (*Hexanchus griseus*)
**Slot:** A primitive deep-sea tank with six gills and glowing blue-green eyes — a living dinosaur.
- **Habitat:** Global (except Arctic/Antarctic), patchy; tropical and temperate waters, continental shelves and slopes, seamounts
- **Depth:** 180–2,500 m; most commonly 200–1,000 m
- **Diet / bait:** Generalist — fish, rays, chimaeras, squid, crabs, shrimp, seals, marine mammal carrion; bait: oily fish or carrion
- **Size:** Up to ~4.8–5.5 m; females mature at ~4 m
- **IUCN:** Near Threatened (global, decreasing)
- **Facts:** Six gill slits instead of five, and fluorescent blue-green eyes / Females bear litters of 47–108 pups — among the largest of any shark / Puget Sound research shows juveniles stay put for up to 4 years, making seasonal north–south shifts of only 10–25 km
- **Migration:** Strong site fidelity in studied inshore populations (detected at the same site 62% of the time); seasonal depth shifts (shallower in summer). Large-scale adult migrations are essentially unknown — a genuine data gap
- **Flags:** none on core facts

### Velvet belly lanternshark (*Etmopterus spinax*)
**Slot:** A pocket-sized shark with a built-in light show — bioluminescent camouflage on its velvet-black belly.
- **Habitat:** Northeastern Atlantic (Iceland and Norway to Gabon), western Mediterranean; one of the commonest deepwater sharks of the NE Atlantic
- **Depth:** 70–2,490 m; most commonly 200–500 m (juveniles venture into Norwegian fjords as shallow as 35 m)
- **Diet / bait:** Young feed on krill and small bony fish; adults take squid, shrimp, bony fishes — bait: shrimp or small fish
- **Size:** Rarely over 45 cm; max recorded ~60 cm
- **IUCN:** Near Threatened
- **Facts:** Species-specific bioluminescent patterns on its flanks are thought to function in communication; belly photophores provide counter-illumination camouflage / Its glow develops before birth — the yolk sac is fluorescent before the photophores form, suggesting the mother transfers luminescent material / Individuals move into deeper water as they age
- **Migration:** No long-distance migration documented; Icelandic research shows it is a narrow-ranging warm-water (6.3–8°C) stenotherm with females and juveniles concentrated in specific shelf habitat
- **Flags:** IUCN status conflict — a 2024 PLOS ONE paper and Wikipedia list Near Threatened, but older pages (Elasmodiver, 2008/2016 assessments) say Least Concern. Use Near Threatened as the current figure

### Dwarf lanternshark (*Etmopterus perryi*)
**Slot:** The smallest shark on Earth — a glowing minnow-shark the length of your hand.
- **Habitat:** Upper continental slopes off Colombia and Venezuela (southern Caribbean) only
- **Depth:** 283–439 m on the upper slope
- **Diet / bait:** Presumed small midwater organisms like other lanternsharks; bait: tiny fish/shrimp (inferred, not documented)
- **Size:** Max known 20–21.2 cm — the smallest shark in the world
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Named for shark biologist Perry W. Gilbert, described from specimens trawled in 1964 / Has needle-shaped dermal denticles scattered randomly, unlike most sharks' scale-like arrangement / Females gestate only 2–3 pups at a time
- **Migration:** Essentially no data — its entire known range is a small patch of Caribbean slope; no migration evidence
- **Flags:** Depth range conflict — some sources give 230–598 m (STI Caribbean database) vs 283–439 m (Wikipedia). Diet is undocumented; treat bait as inference. Several rival "smallest shark" claims (pygmy shark, spined pygmy shark) reflect difficulty assessing maturity, not settled fact

### Kitefin shark (*Dalatias licha*)
**Slot:** The largest glowing animal on Earth — a slow-cruising predator that bites chunks out of animals bigger than itself.
- **Habitat:** Sporadic worldwide; outer continental and insular shelves and slopes in warm-temperate and tropical waters (Atlantic, Mediterranean, Indian Ocean, Pacific)
- **Depth:** 37–1,800 m; usually 200–600 m
- **Diet / bait:** Bony fishes, smaller sharks, skates, squid, crustaceans — and bites cut from larger animals like its cookiecutter cousin; bait: fish
- **Size:** Typically 1.0–1.4 m; max ~1.8 m
- **IUCN:** Near Threatened (global); Endangered in European and Mediterranean waters
- **Facts:** The largest luminous vertebrate on record / A targeted Azores fishery for its squalene-rich liver oil collapsed in the 1990s — a textbook case of deep-sea shark vulnerability / Mediterranean research suggests males and females segregate spatially
- **Migration:** Essentially unknown — little is known about its movements; no long-distance migration documented
- **Flags:** IUCN conflict — the most recent global assessment cited in literature (IUCN 2020, via Bottaro et al. 2023) is Near Threatened, but Wikipedia and some papers say Vulnerable (2018 Finucci assessment). Regional EN status is consistent

### Pacific sleeper shark (*Somniosus pacificus*)
**Slot:** A giant stealth hunter that glides through the dark without moving a muscle.
- **Habitat:** North Pacific (Japan to Mexico, Bering Sea, Gulf of Alaska), continental shelves and slopes in Arctic and temperate waters
- **Depth:** Surface to 2,000 m; most abundant 200–700 m near the bottom
- **Diet / bait:** Giant Pacific octopus, squid, flatfish, rockfish, crabs, Pacific salmon, seals and juvenile sea lions, carrion — bait: oily fish or octopus
- **Size:** Up to ~4.4 m; possibly 7+ m
- **IUCN:** Near Threatened (reclassified from Data Deficient in 2021)
- **Facts:** Glides with almost no body movement or hydrodynamic noise, making it a stealth predator — feeding uses suction and a characteristic rolling motion of the head / No pregnant female has ever been found, suggesting they pup at extreme depths / Diet broadens with size: small sharks eat mostly fish, large ones take marine mammals
- **Migration:** Documented diel vertical migration (up into the water column at night, to the bottom by day) plus seasonal depth shifts — average capture depth is shallowest in June, deepest in December
- **Flags:** Some older sources still list Data Deficient; current (2021) assessment is Near Threatened with an estimated 20–29% decline. Southern-hemisphere records are likely misidentified *S. antarcticus*

### Spiny dogfish (*Squalus acanthias*)
**Slot:** A pack-hunting, mildly venomous shark that travels thousands of kilometres and pregnancies last two years.
- **Habitat:** Temperate and subarctic waters worldwide (except tropics and poles); Atlantic, southern South America, South Africa, Australia/NZ, North Pacific (now *S. suckleyi*)
- **Depth:** Intertidal to ~900 m; usually coastal and demersal in 7–15°C water
- **Diet / bait:** Opportunistic — small squid, fish, crabs, shrimp, jellyfish, sea cucumbers; bait: squid or fish chunks
- **Size:** Females to ~1–1.24 m; males smaller (~60–100 cm)
- **IUCN:** Vulnerable (global); Endangered in the Northeast Atlantic
- **Facts:** Has venomous spines in front of each dorsal fin and no anal fin — a classic identification pair / Gestation of 18–24 months is among the longest of any vertebrate / Travels in huge schools (up to thousands), segregated by size and sex, on migrations of thousands of kilometres
- **Migration:** Highly migratory — documented seasonal migrations of thousands of kilometres (north–south and inshore–offshore); little mixing between northern and southern hemisphere populations
- **Flags:** IUCN conflict — Wikipedia now lists Vulnerable globally, but the Shark Research Institute page and older assessments say Near Threatened globally / Endangered NE Atlantic. Also a taxonomic flag: North Pacific populations were split off in 2010 as *Squalus suckleyi*, so some Pacific records under *acanthias* are outdated

### Pygmy shark (*Euprotomicrus bispinatus*)
**Slot:** The second-smallest shark — a hand-sized deepwater predator that rides an elevator 1,500 m every day.
- **Habitat:** Warm-temperate and subtropical open ocean worldwide (Atlantic, Indian, Pacific); pelagic, not bottom-dwelling
- **Depth:** Surface to ~1,500 m; days spent deep, nights near the surface
- **Diet / bait:** Deepwater squid, bony fishes, crustaceans — bait: squid or small fish
- **Size:** Max ~27 cm (females ~25 cm, males ~22 cm)
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** Makes a daily vertical migration of over 1,500 m — ~500 m by day, up to ~200 m at night, following its prey / Its paddle-shaped, nearly symmetrical tail and black top / glowing belly make it a textbook mid-ocean cruiser / Swallows its own shed teeth like the cookiecutter shark
- **Migration:** The headline pattern is extreme diel vertical migration (>1,500 m each way daily); sometimes gathers in groups during these movements; no horizontal migration data
- **Flags:** The Shark Research Institute page says "Not Evaluated" — outdated; Wikipedia and the Australian fish report list Least Concern (Burgess assessment). Claims of daytime depths to 9,938 m appear in one source and are almost certainly unreliable; use surface–1,500 m

### Small-spotted catshark (*Scyliorhinus canicula*)
**Slot:** Europe's most familiar shark — the sandy-bottomed, egg-laying neighbour of every UK beachgoer.
- **Habitat:** NE Atlantic continental shelf (Norway and British Isles to Senegal) and Mediterranean; sandy, gravelly or muddy bottoms
- **Depth:** A few metres to 400–550 m; most common 50–250 m on the shelf
- **Diet / bait:** Benthic invertebrates — crabs, shrimp, hermit crabs, molluscs, polychaete worms — plus small fish; bait: crab or fish chunks
- **Size:** Usually 60–70 cm; max ~1 m
- **IUCN:** Least Concern
- **Facts:** One of the most abundant sharks in European waters — and an egg-layer: females lay "mermaid's purse" capsules that anchor to seaweed, developing for 5–11 months / Nocturnal: males rest on the seabed by day while females hide in shallow-water shelters / Sandpaper-rough skin (it's the origin of the name "rough-hound")
- **Migration:** High site fidelity — tagging shows adults stay local, recaptured close to release points; matures shift shoreward, returning to the shelf edge to breed. No long-distance migration
- **Flags:** One tagging paper's distance figures are garbled in secondary sources ("did not move 424 km" alongside "maximum 256 km") — the consistent finding is strong local site fidelity, so report that conservatively

## Catalogue notes

- **Species cut for lack of good data:** none. All 45 candidates held up to research. Two dossiers are thinner than the rest and flagged as such: horn shark (*Heterodontus francisci*, IUCN Data Deficient) and megamouth (*Megachasma pelagios*, ~300 individuals ever recorded — several facts inferred from a handful of specimens). Avery may want to cut or keep these on charm alone.
- **IUCN conflicts are the norm, not the exception:** many online sources still show pre-2019/2021 statuses. Each dossier's Flags note where the current assessment differs from what older pages say. When writing field-guide copy, use the status in the dossier, not the first Google result.
- **Regional vs global status:** porbeagle, spiny dogfish, kitefin, and zebra shark have regional assessments that differ sharply from global ones — worth surfacing in-game where relevant.
- **Not in this catalogue:** the six current roster sharks (nurse, thresher, whale, goblin, tiger, sand tiger) — already built.
- **Suggested first wave** (not decided — Avery's call): Galápagos shark → Galápagos Islands teaser; great white → South Africa teaser. Both regions are currently "surveys coming soon" and these two species are the payoff.
